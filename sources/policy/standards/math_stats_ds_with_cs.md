# Overview

The AOE recommendation for statewide graduation requirements in mathematics is for 4 credits of math, consisting of 1 credit algebra, 1 credit geometry, 1 credit statistics or data science, and 1 elective. This document outlines how districts could implement these credits.

This proposal lays out a pathway for high school math that provides greater flexibility for students pursuing flexible pathway, makes math more relevant for students, empowers students to utilize their mathematical reasoning to positively affect the world, and makes computer science a legitimate part of Vermont student’s education.

It does this by broadening what can count toward the statistics and data science credit and the elective by recognizing the CSTA computer science standards alongside the Common Core mathematics standards. While only six states had adopted K-12 CS standards in 2017, nearly all states now have CS standards, with [42 states identified with standards in 2024](https://reimaginingcs.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Standards-Comparison.pdf). States that previously realized the importance of computer science in K12 education are now adding it as a graduation requirement, this now includes; Alabama, Arkansas, Indiana, Louisiana, Nebraska, Nevada, North Carolina, North Dakota, Rhode Island, South Carolina, Tennessee and West Virginia. ([‘Significant momentum’ for computer science curriculum \| K-12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/news/significant-momentum-computer-science-curriculum-ai/748103/)).

The introduction of Artificial Intelligence has made the need for computer science standards even more essential. Without a grounding in the statistics, probability, and computer science concepts that sit at the heart of AI systems, students will be completely unprepared for the world that they are entering. Without this grounding, students are apt to anthropomorphize the systems and AI as magical, when at their heart AI systems are based on technologies that can be taught in high school math and computer science classes. Equipped with the proper knowledge, students can fully leverage these powerful tools to be more successful in their careers and to achieve their goals.

## Statistics and Data Science Credit (includes Algebra II in many cases)

The statistics and data science credit is designed to respond to a shift in the type of mathematics that is most relevant to students’ future careers and civic life. For many students a solid knowledge of statistics and data science will be more relevant than a pathway toward calculus; this includes college-bound students. A knowledge of statistics is critical to being able to make informed civic decisions and all modern businesses leverage data to make decisions and automate processes.

This credit can be satisfied by a course in statistics, a course in data science, or Algebra II if the Algebra II course includes coverage of statistics and probability. Common Core’s Appendix A places the Algebra II statistics standards (S-ID.4 and the S-IC inference cluster) in Algebra II itself, so many Algebra II courses carry this credit directly.

In this version of the recommendation, a course can also satisfy the credit by significantly covering the CSTA high school Data and Analysis standards together with a reduced set of essential statistics standards described later in this document. This makes room for data-focused science courses, career and technical education (CTE) courses with a strong data component, and courses that analyze data through computing, while still guaranteeing the statistical reasoning every graduate needs. The two ways to satisfy the credit are set out in full under The Statistics and Data Science Credit below.

Many schools incorporate most of the Common Core statistics clusters into their existing algebra and geometry course series. Districts can take advantage of this when designing their courses to satisfy the graduation requirements. Oregon’s 2+1 model uses this same decision. This leads to two options for districts.

**Option 1 — Embed some of the Common Core statistics clusters into the first two years of math courses.**

Many algebra and geometry courses embed statistics concepts while also addressing algebra and geometry, and this provides schools more breathing space for a third credit of statistics or data science. Because students have covered many statistics concepts in algebra and geometry, a greater variety of courses can satisfy the data science credit. This approach borrows from [Oregon’s 2+1 model](https://www.nctm.org/uploadedFiles/Publications/Features/HS-Math-State-Story-Oregon.pdf) as well as Common Core Appendix A.

**Option 2 — Cover the Common Core statistics clusters in a dedicated credit offering.**

If the district does not embed any statistics and probability into its algebra and geometry offerings, then it must address these as part of the statistics and data science credit as a stand-alone course.

## Math Elective

The math elective is defined to create additional flexibility. Because the required Common Core standards are covered in the algebra, geometry, and statistics/data science credits, the elective is free to recognize sustained mathematical reasoning wherever it genuinely occurs, including in computer science courses and computationally rigorous CTE programs.

A course can satisfy the elective in either of two ways: by significantly covering high school Common Core mathematics, or by significantly covering the CSTA high school standards for Algorithms and Design, Programming, and/or Data and Analysis. The second way recognizes that computer science, taught with real rigor, is sustained mathematical reasoning in an applied form. The full definition, including how the elective is distinguished from the statistics and data science credit, appears under The Math Elective Credit below.

## Computer Science Standards in Vermont

Vermont has adopted the Common Core mathematics standards but has not adopted computer science standards. The absence of computer science standards is a real disadvantage for Vermont students: without them, few districts maintain a genuine computer science curriculum, and computer science positions are among the first cut in tight budget years. Recognizing computer science within the mathematics graduation requirements is one practical way to give these courses a durable place in the curriculum.

Vermont has adopted the ISTE Student Standards (Internation Society for Technology in Education). The ISTE standards were never meant to be standards for what students should learn, instead they are standards for how students should leverage technology to support their learning process while in school. This has led to confusion in Vermont schools, because the ISTE student standards are the only thing they are able to tie digital literacy skills to. The CSTA computer science standards are designed as the standards for what every K12 student should learn, not just for those pursuing careers in computing fields.

[Most states have adopted variations of the Computer Science Teachers Association (CSTA) standards](https://reimaginingcs.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Standards-Comparison.pdf). The CSTA is releasing updated standards in July 2026 that include revisions to cover artificial intelligence.

This document treats the CSTA standards as the computer science standards Vermont intends to use. It references them here so that computer science and data science can be recognized as forms of applied mathematics. This document is based on draft version 3 of the CSTA standards; the specific references in this document should be confirmed against the final version, and the question of formally adopting computer science standards in Vermont is a separate decision that has not yet been made.

# The Statistics and Data Science Credit

## Two ways a course can satisfy this credit

A course satisfies the statistics and data science credit if it does either of the following:

1.  It significantly covers the Common Core statistics and probability standards (the S-ID, S-IC, and S-CP clusters, excluding the advanced (+) standards); or

2.  It significantly covers the CSTA high school Data and Analysis standards *and, in addition, the essential math common core statistical standards* listed below.

The first describes a statistics course, an AP Statistics course, or a data science course built on the common core mathematics standards. The second describes a data-focused course, including science and CTE courses, that teaches students to investigate real data using computational tools, paired with the statistical reasoning those courses do not otherwise guarantee.

The meaning of “significant coverage” is discussed at the end of this section. First, we address the required statistics standards

## Required statistics standards

Vermont has adopted the Common Core State Standards for Mathematics (AOE report on graduation requirements, Appendix C). Common Core distinguishes standards expected of **all** college- and career-ready students from advanced standards flagged (+), which the common core describes as additional mathematics for students heading into calculus, advanced statistics, or discrete math, *not* to be expected of every graduate.

S-MD (Using Probability to Make Decisions) and S-CP.8–9 (Advanced counting and probability rules) are appropriate for elective courses or courses that satisfy the data science requirement, but they need not be part of every student’s pathway.

| **Cluster**                                               | **Required**              | **Advanced (+)**    |
|-----------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------|---------------------|
| **S-ID — Interpreting Categorical & Quantitative Data**   | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 | — (none)            |
| **S-IC — Making Inferences & Justifying Conclusions**     | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6          | — (none)            |
| **S-CP — Conditional Probability & Rules of Probability** | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7       | 8, 9                |
| **S-MD — Using Probability to Make Decisions**            | — (none)                  | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 |

Many of the required statistics and probability standards are taught in existing algebra and geometry courses. Common Core Appendix A lays out sample model course series showing how the standards can be distributed. Its traditional Algebra I, Geometry, Algebra II series covers:

| **Statistics content**                                                                    | **Appendix A home** |
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------|
| S-ID.1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 — descriptive stats, scatterplots, linear models, correlation | Algebra I           |
| S-CP.1–7 — conditional probability, two-way tables                                        | Geometry            |
| S-ID.4 — normal distribution                                                              | Algebra II          |
| S-IC.1–6 — sampling, margin of error, experiments, evaluating reports                     | Algebra II          |

EdReports, the best-known reviewer of high-quality instructional materials (HQIM), only endorses curricula that cover the full set of high school statistics and probability standards. In many Algebra II programs, the statistics and probability sections are placed at the end of the year, with some teachers dropping them based on the pacing of the class. The guidance here ensures that statistics and probability get proper coverage in all students' pathways.

The math pathways districts design should give every student substantial, central work in probability and statistics. How districts distribute these standards influences the flexibility they have in assigning credit. The line between required and advanced is the one Common Core already draws: it is exactly the statistics Common Core expects of every graduate, and it operationalizes the ASA–NCTM position the AoE report cites (p.19): statistics should be accessible to all students, not limited to advanced mathematics pathways.

In Vermont’s recommended requirements, Algebra II is an *elective* math credit, not guaranteed for all. So, the typical Algebra II statistics standards (**S-ID.4 and all of S-IC**) cannot be assumed to be embedded into the required Algebra I + Geometry sequence without compressing those courses. They fall to the third credit under either option.

## The second way: CSTA Data and Analysis

The CSTA high school Data and Analysis standards describe the work of a data investigation: organizing and cleaning data, building and reading visualizations, running and evaluating simulations, and judging what the results can and cannot support. A variety of courses can be designed that makes this work central, this could look like; a data-focused science course, a CTE course built around an operational dataset, or a course that analyzes data through computing without necessarily using a formal language like R or Python, can satisfy this credit through these standards.

| **Standard**  | **What it asks students to do**                                                                                                                           |
|---------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **HS-DAA-22** | Use a digital tool to generate data that fits certain parameters for use in simulations.                                                                  |
| **HS-DAA-23** | Create a data dictionary describing the names and types of attributes, allowable values and ranges, and the relationships between variables in a dataset. |
| **HS-DAA-24** | Use a digital tool to clean and organize text-based data.                                                                                                 |
| **HS-DAA-25** | Evaluate different approaches to verifying that data is consistent with expected types, values, and ranges.                                               |
| **HS-DAA-26** | Use computational tools to create visualizations of multivariate data sets to answer a question, classify, or make predictions.                           |
| **HS-DAA-27** | Evaluate the results of data simulations and visualizations to help answer questions and inform decisions, including identifying limitations.             |

*Note: Two further standards in this strand, HS-DAA-28 and HS-DAA-29, address the societal and ethical impacts of data science. They are valuable, but significant coverage for satisfying this credit should center on the investigation standards above rather than be met by the impacts standards alone. Most dedicated data science courses should include these standards*

But the CSTA standards are not sufficient to satisfy the statistics and data science credit requirement.

## The essential statistics standards every graduate needs

The CSTA Data and Analysis standards teach students to organize, visualize, and investigate data, but on their own they do not teach the statistical reasoning a person needs to judge what data can and cannot show. CSTA leaves this out on purpose, to avoid duplicating mathematics standards that states have already adopted. The result is that a student could complete a strong data course and still be unable to tell the difference between correlation and causation, or to judge whether a study supports a claim being made from it. These are exactly the skills an informed citizen relies on, and the situations in which data is most often misused.

For that reason, a course qualifying through the CSTA Data and Analysis standards must also cover the four Common Core standards below. A course qualifying through the first way already includes them.

| **Standard** | **What it asks students to do**                                                                                                                    |
|--------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **S-ID.9**   | Distinguish between correlation and causation.                                                                                                     |
| **S-IC.1**   | Understand statistics as a process for making inferences about a population based on a random sample.                                              |
| **S-IC.3**   | Recognize the purposes of and differences among sample surveys, experiments, and observational studies; explain how randomization relates to each. |
| **S-IC.6**   | Evaluate reports based on data.                                                                                                                    |

**These four are a starting point.** The specific list should be confirmed with the Vermont Council of Teachers of Mathematics, the computer science teaching community, and university partners. That conversation is part of finalizing this recommendation, and it depends in part on the adoption of computer science standards.

## Mathematical practices and the investigation cycle

However a course qualifies, the **statistics and data science credit** must be grounded in the Common Core Standards for Mathematical Practice, which the Vermont State Board of Education has adopted:

- Make sense of problems and persevere in solving them.

- Reason abstractly and quantitatively.

- Construct viable arguments and critique the reasoning of others.

- Model with mathematics.

- Use appropriate tools strategically.

- Attend to precision.

- Look for and make use of structure.

- Look for and express regularity in repeated reasoning.

To further define the work of the credit, we look to [GAISE II](https://www.nctm.org/uploadedFiles/GAISEIIPreK-12_Full.pdf) and its statistical-investigation cycle: formulate a question, collect or consider data, analyze, and interpret. A qualifying course makes that cycle, or the equivalent mathematical reasoning over data, its central, recurring work, not an occasional activity.

## How much statistics each option requires

The design of the first two years of mathematics shapes how much statistics content must be carried by the third credit.

| **Statistics content**                            | **Option 1 (embed in Algebra & Geometry)** | **Option 2 (no embedding)** |
|---------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| S-ID.1–3, 5–9 — descriptive stats + linear models | Algebra credit                             | Third credit                |
| S-CP.1–7 — conditional probability                | Geometry credit                            | Third credit                |
| S-ID.4 — normal distribution                      | Third credit                               | Third credit                |
| S-IC.1–6 — inference                              | Third credit                               | Third credit                |

**Meaningful coverage, not complete coverage.** Because Algebra II is not required for graduation in many Vermont high schools, complete coverage of the statistics and probability standards was never guaranteed. Requiring complete coverage could narrow students’ options, since many data science courses lean toward the applied use of technology and may not formalize every standard. In particular, the formal-inference standards S-IC.4 and S-IC.5 (developing a margin of error, and testing whether a difference is significant) should not be treated as something every qualifying course must reach. The goal for the credit is substantial, central coverage of probability and statistics, not a complete checklist.

A widely used data science course makes the trade-off concrete. [California’s Introduction to Data Science (IDS)](https://www.introdatascience.org/wp-content/uploads/California-Common-Core-Mathematics-Standards-addressed-by-IDS.pdf) maps to S-ID.4 and S-IC.1, 2, 3, and 6, but not S-IC.4 or 5; it reaches inference informally. A faithful Algebra II course or AP Statistics does cover S-IC.4 and 5. We therefore treat the formal-inference standards as content some versions of this credit reach and others need not.

## On “significant coverage”

Defining “significant coverage” precisely, how many standards in a cluster or strand a course must address, and at what depth, is work to be done with stakeholders rather than settled here. The Agency’s intent is conceptual: a qualifying course makes statistics, probability, or data investigation a central and recurring part of the year, not an incidental topic. The four essential statistics standards above are the one firm requirement for the second way; the remaining thresholds should be set together with the Vermont Council of Teachers of Mathematics, the computer science teaching community, and university experts. Computer science standards have not yet been formally adopted, and this part of the recommendation is expected to evolve through that review.

# The Math Elective Credit

The fourth math credit is an elective. The elective does not need to carry a specific content checklist. That frees students to pursue courses where they apply mathematical reasoning in domains that are relevant and interesting to them. This is the credit that lets a computer science course or a computationally rigorous CTE program count toward student’s requirements for graduation. This is consistent with the AOE recommendation that the elective be defined “based on college/career pathway” (p.19) and that CTE learning satisfy content-area requirements where it aligns (SBR 2383.3).

## Two ways a course can satisfy the elective

A course satisfies the elective math credit if it does either of the following:

1.  It significantly covers high school Common Core mathematics — the Standards for Mathematical Practice applied to real mathematical content in functions, algebra, number and quantity, or modeling; or

2.  It significantly covers the CSTA high school standards for Algorithms and Design, Programming, and/or Data and Analysis.

In both cases, the test is the same in spirit: mathematical reasoning is the **central and sustained** work of the course across the year, not an incidental feature of an occasional lesson. The first way recognizes that reasoning in its Common Core form. The second recognizes it in its applied computational form.

### Elective Qualification: CSTA Algorithms and Design, Programming, and Data and Analysis

The Algorithms and Design and Programming strands of the CSTA standards carry the logic, abstraction, and structure that map back to the Common Core functions and algebra standards. The Data and Analysis strand is the same strand named in the statistics and data science credit, a data investigation is mathematical reasoning whether a district places it in the data science credit or the elective. The other two strands of the CSTA standards (Systems & Security, Computing & Society) do not count toward a math elective.

As with the statistics credit, the precise threshold for “significant coverage” is to be set with stakeholders. The CSTA standards have not been adopted in Vermont.

## Computer science, CTE, and STEAM courses

A computationally rigorous computer science, CTE, or STEAM course can reach a math credit in more than one way, depending on where its rigor lives:

- If the central work is a **statistical or data investigation** (formulate, collect or consider, analyze, interpret real data) and it covers the essential statistics standards, it can count as the statistics and data science credit, or as an elective.

- If the central work is **reasoning over algorithms, logic, functions, abstraction, or computational structure**, it counts as an elective.

- If such reasoning is only **incidental** — the course uses numbers or tools but never asks students to build, trace, or justify a model or procedure — it is a valuable general elective but does not satisfy a math credit.

Only courses in which the mathematics is incidental fail to qualify. A course with sustained quantitative or computational rigor lands in one math credit or the other.

## One course, one credit

A single course earns one credit. When a course could satisfy more than one of these requirements — for example, a data science course that covers both the statistics standards and the CSTA Data and Analysis standards — the district assigns it to one credit. The same coursework is not counted twice.

It qualifies as the math elective in either case.

**Source.** [courseinfo.code.org/csaif](https://courseinfo.code.org/csaif/)

**Description.** A free, modular introductory high school course built from short three-week units spanning computer systems, introductory programming, networks, cybersecurity, an introduction to data, and artificial intelligence, with a full-year option that adds JavaScript and web apps. This course was redesigned partly in response to how programming is changing with AI: students leverage AI systems for some development work. That does not preclude it from counting as a math elective. Programming is not the only indicator of mathematical reasoning; what matters is the application of mathematical concepts and ways of thinking, which can occur even when students do not write all the code themselves.

# Appendix A –CSTA Standards Crosswalk

The argument that some computer science courses contain significant mathematical learning to count as a math credit relies on a crosswalk analysis of the CSTA standards to the Common Core standards. The standards complement each other; they are not meant to overlap. The CSTA standards for three out of five domains; "Algorithm and Design", "Programming", and "Data & Analysis" all involve components of applied mathematics.

This crosswalk is meant to be done once, centrally. A district that has already aligned a course to the CSTA standards can read a math-elective determination directly from this appendix, without re-deriving the Common Core mapping course by course. As elsewhere in this document, the CSTA standards are referenced provisionally; they have not been adopted in Vermont.

## Test for math elective requirements

Two conditions decide whether a course qualifies as a math elective, and they are the same test used throughout this document.

1.  **The mathematics is at high school level** — the functions, algebra, modeling, and statistics of Common Core high school, not the arithmetic and percentages of earlier grades.

2.  **The mathematics is the sustained central work of the course and is captured as such in the learning outcomes.** The *topic* of the course need not be "mathematics." This is what allows interdisciplinary, computational, and career-connected courses to qualify. But the *learning outcomes* must be mathematical.

The Standards for Mathematical Practice run through all of computer science — abstraction, structure, precision, and repeated reasoning are everywhere in it. Practice alone is not enough to make a course a math elective; many rigorous courses exercise the mathematical practices. The overlap that matters is where those practices operate on genuine high school mathematical *content*. That overlap is what the tables below identify.

Within each of the three domains (algorithm and design, programming, data and analysis), a core group of standards maps to Common Core and represent applied mathematics. A second group addresses the ethical, collaborative, AI-literacy, and societal dimensions of computing. That second group is the *complement* — important to a computing course, but not where its mathematics lives, and by design not overlapping the mathematics standards.

## Algorithms and Design

The mathematical core of this domain is the design and analysis of algorithms. Writing a procedure with sequence, selection, and iteration is building a function and reasoning about repeated structure. Evaluating an algorithm for efficiency and correctness is comparing rates of growth and justifying that a procedure does what it claims. Optimizing with abstractions is the mathematical habit of finding and exploiting structure. The remaining standards concern AI literacy and the ethics of algorithmic systems.

## Reading the crosswalk

In each of the three domains, the standards that define its central work map onto high school Common Core mathematics. The standards on ethics, collaboration, AI literacy, and the societal impact of computing do not, by design. This is why "significant coverage" for a math elective should center on CSTA standards that are cross walked to HS CCSS-M standards.

A course whose CSTA-aligned learning outcomes land substantially in these mathematical standards is doing sustained, high-school-level applied mathematics, and qualifies as a math elective. A course that touches them only in passing does not. Appendix B applies the same crosswalk strategy to the JumpStart National Standards for Personal Financial Education.

It is interesting to note that the common core math standards lack coverage of the ethical impacts of algorithms. This may be because these standards have not been updated since their release in 2010. The Common Core Mathematic standards can complement nicely with the CSTA standards in this regard.

# Appendix B – Jump\$tart Standards crosswalk

Appendix A showed that the core computer science standards map onto high school Common Core mathematics. This appendix applies the same reasoning to personal finance and reaches a different result. This answers a specific question: why can some computer science courses count as a math elective when a typical financial literacy course cannot?

Vermont is moving to require a half credit of personal financial literacy for graduation, so it is worth being precise about what a personal finance course does and does not satisfy. Two principles decide the question, and both come from elsewhere in this document. The first is the learning-outcomes test: a course counts as a math elective when its mathematics is at high school level and is the sustained central work of the course, captured as such in the learning outcomes, the topic need not be mathematics, but the outcomes must be. The second is the no double dipping rule: a learning experience earns credit, and the same seat time cannot be applied to multiple credits. Personal finance is valuable, and is now its own graduation requirement; the question here is only whether, and when, it is also a *math* credit.

## The Jump\$tart standards

The JumpStart National Standards for Personal Financial Education organize personal finance into six areas: Earning Income, Spending, Saving, Investing, Managing Credit, and Managing Risk. High school mathematics does appear in them — compound interest and future value in Saving and Investing, APR and the cost of a loan in Managing Credit, expected value in Managing Risk. Two things limit how far that mathematics goes.

First, the quantitative benchmarks cluster in the grade-8 band. The benchmarks that ask students to calculate future value, use the Rule of 72, or compute an APR are grade-8 benchmarks. The grade-12 (high school) benchmarks are largely "describe," "compare," "explain," and "evaluate," and the few numeric ones sit at arithmetic level.

Second, even where Common Core content appears, it is a tool in service of a financial decision, not the object of study. Students use a percentage or a formula to reach a consumer conclusion; they do not build, solve, or model.

| **JumpStart area** | **Common Core mathematics that appears**                  | **Level and role**                  |
|--------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| I. Earning Income  | N-Q percentages (tax, gross vs. net)                      | below HS; incidental                |
| II\. Spending      | N-Q unit rates (budgets, price comparison)                | below HS; incidental                |
| III\. Saving       | F-LE / F-BF exponential (compound interest, future value) | grade-8 benchmarks; brief           |
| IV\. Investing     | F-LE growth (returns); S-MD expected value                | grade-8 / conceptual; not developed |
| V. Managing Credit | N-Q, F-LE (APR, total interest paid)                      | grade-8; applied briefly            |
| VI\. Managing Risk | S-CP / S-MD probability, expected value                   | conceptual; not developed           |

Across all six areas, the high school learning outcomes are financial-decision outcomes. The mathematics is real but thin, mostly below high school level, and never the sustained work. On the learning-outcomes test, JumpStart-aligned personal finance does not, on its own, meet either prong.

## No double dipping

The learning-outcomes test asks whether a course *could* be mathematics. A second rule limits when that matters. A 1 credit experience (E.g. a year long course) should only count for a total of 1 credit of graduation requirements.

A one-semester personal finance course used to meet Vermont's half-credit financial literacy requirement therefore cannot also count as half a credit of mathematics. Financial literacy and mathematics are separate graduation requirements, and the credit cannot be spent twice. This is the same rule applied to computer science and data science courses elsewhere in this document: when a course could satisfy more than one requirement, the district assigns it to one or it is set to one based on a per student basis.

The practical effect is that for most students the semester financial literacy course will be spent satisfying the financial literacy requirement, and whatever mathematics it contains is not available a second time as math credit. A finance course reaches a math credit only when it is a *different, additional* course whose learning outcomes are mathematical — not the one already meeting the financial literacy requirement. Please note, this would still allow a full year one credit course to apply .5 credit to one requirement and .5 credit to a different requirement.

## Course examples

Two courses from one provider, NGPF, show how both principles resolve in practice.

### NGPF Semester Course

**Source.** [ngpf.org/courses/semester-course](https://www.ngpf.org/courses/semester-course/)

**Description.** NGPF's flagship personal finance course, described as covering the essential topics to become "a financially capable student." Its eleven units span behavioral economics, banking, investing, credit and managing credit, paying for college, career, insurance, taxes, budgeting, and consumer skills, aligned to the National Standards for Personal Financial Education. Quantitative work appears in a minority of optional activities — for example, calculating a loan payment, a mortgage cost, or tax withholding.

**Assessment – Does not qualify as a math credit.** This is the idealized version of a financial-literacy graduation requirement, and it is a strong personal finance course. Its learning outcomes are financial capability and consumer decision-making; mathematics shows up only as an occasional tool, at arithmetic and percentage level, and is not part of the stated learning outcomes. The learning-outcomes test is not met. And the one-course-one-credit rule settles it independently: a semester of this course used to meet the half-credit financial literacy requirement has spent its seat time and cannot also be claimed as math. On either principle, it is a financial literacy course, not a math elective.

### NGPF Financial Algebra

**Source.** [ngpf.org/math/financial-algebra](https://www.ngpf.org/math/financial-algebra/)

**Description.** NGPF presents this as a full-year math course. It has a syllabus, a unit exam for each of ten units, a diagnostic, and a final. It is described as maintaining "deep mathematical rigor" and aligned to Common Core Mathematics. Each unit pairs a finance topic with a mathematics concept: fundamentals of algebra and functions, linear equations, systems of equations and inequalities, exponential functions and logarithms, modeling and recursive sequences, statistics, and probability, with finance (taxes, checking, saving, budgeting, investing, credit, college, insurance) as the setting. Its mathematical spine is essentially a first- or second-year algebra scope and sequence delivered through financial contexts.

**Assessment – Qualifies as mathematics** By its learning outcomes this is a mathematics course. Students write and graph linear and exponential equations, solve systems, model with functions and recursive sequences, and work with statistics and probability — mapping broadly onto Common Core F-IF, F-BF, F-LE, A-CED, A-REI, S-ID, S-IC, and S-CP. It clears the learning-outcomes test for a math elective comfortably. For a district to use this as their “algebra 1 credit” they will need to do a careful curriculum mapping to the appropriate standards and ensure alignment with the other courses in their sequence to ensure that they have a coordinated curriculum with complete coverage.

**Conclusion**

Looking at two courses, both from the same provider, in the same subject area tell a consistent story. Determining credit does not rest on the topic or subject area. Both courses are in the domain of finance. The key is to focus on the mathematical standards that the course meets and you can most quickly determine that by analyzing the course’s learning outcomes. A financial forecasting or financial algebra course, whose learning outcomes are mathematical, can be a math credit. A financial literacy course cannot, because its outcomes are financial, and because the credit it earns is already committed to the financial literacy requirement. The same discipline that lets a mathematically rigorous computer science course count as applied mathematics keeps a financial literacy course in its own, equally required, place. This same discipline equally rules out computer science courses that are not sufficiently mathematically focused.
