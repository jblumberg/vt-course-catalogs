# Overview

The AOE recommendation for statewide graduation requirements in mathematics is for 4 credits of math, consisting of 1 credit algebra, 1 credit geometry, 1 credit statistics or data science and 1 elective. This document outlines how districts could implement these credits.

## Statistics & Data Science Credit (includes Algebra II in many cases)

The statistics and data science credit is designed to respond to a shift in the type of mathematics that is most relevant to students’ future careers and civic life. For many students a solid knowledge of statistics and data science will be more relevant than a pathways toward calculus, this includes college bound students.

This third credit can be satisfied by a course in statistics, a course in data science, or Algebra II if the Algebra II course includes coverage of statistics and probability. Common Core’s Appendix A places the Algebra II statistics standards (S-ID.4 and the S-IC inference cluster) in Algebra II itself, so many Algebra II courses carry this credit directly.

Many schools incorporate most of the common core math’s statistics clusters into their existing Algebra and Geometry course series. Districts can take advantage of this in their design of their courses to satisfy the graduation credit requirements. Oregon’s 2+1 model uses this same decision. This leads to two options for districts:  
  
Option \#1 – Embed some of common core math’s statistics clusters into the first two years of math courses

Many Algebra & Geometry courses embed statistics concepts while also addressing Algebra and Geometry, and this provides schools more breathing space for a 3<sup>rd</sup> credit of statistics or data science. Because students have coverage of many statistics concepts in Algebra and Geometry, a greater variety of courses can satisfy the data science credit requirement. This approach borrows from [Oregon’s 2+1 model](https://www.nctm.org/uploadedFiles/Publications/Features/HS-Math-State-Story-Oregon.pdf) as well as Common Core Math Appendix A.

<span id="_Toc233288547" class="anchor"></span>Option \#2 – Cover the Common Core Math Statistic’s clusters in a dedicated credit offering

If the district does not embed statistics and probability into their algebra and geometry offerings, then they must address these as part of their statistics and data science credit.

## Math Elective

The math elective is defined to create additional flexibility. Because the required Common Core standards are covered in the algebra, geometry, and statistics/data science credits, the elective is free to recognize sustained mathematical reasoning wherever it genuinely occurs, including in computer science courses and computationally rigorous career and technical education (CTE) programs. Rather than naming approved courses, the credit is defined by a standards floor: a course qualifies when the Standards for Mathematical Practice are its central, sustained work and that work engages real mathematical content. This casts a deliberately wide net including courses containing applied math. The full definition, including how the elective is distinguished from the statistics/data science credit, appears under Applying Standards below.

# Applying Standards

## Required Statistics Standards

Vermont has adopted the Common Core State Standards for Mathematics (AoE report, Appendix C). Common Core distinguishes standards expected of **all** college- and career-ready students from advanced standards flagged (+), which the standards describe as additional mathematics for students heading into calculus, advanced statistics, or discrete math, *not* to be expected of every graduate.

| **Cluster**                                               | **Required**              | **Advanced (+)**    |
|-----------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------|---------------------|
| **S-ID** — Interpreting Categorical & Quantitative Data   | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 | — (none)            |
| **S-IC** — Making Inferences & Justifying Conclusions     | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6          | — (none)            |
| **S-CP** — Conditional Probability & Rules of Probability | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7       | 8, 9                |
| **S-MD** — Using Probability to Make Decisions            | — (none)                  | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 |

Many of the required statistics and probability standards are taught in existing algebra and geometry courses. In fact, Common Core Math Appendix A lays out sample model course series mapping out how standards can be applied. Their version of a traditional Algebra I, Geometry, Algebra II series covers:

| **Statistics content**                                                                    | **CCSS Appendix A home** |
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------|
| S-ID.1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 — descriptive stats, scatterplots, linear models, correlation | **Algebra I**            |
| S-CP.1–7 — conditional probability, two-way tables                                        | **Geometry**             |
| S-ID.4 — normal distribution                                                              | **Algebra II**           |
| S-IC.1–6 — sampling, margin of error, experiments, evaluating reports                     | **Algebra II**           |

The math pathways that districts design should give every student substantial, central work in probability and statistics. The way districts distribute these standards will influence the flexibility they have in assigning credit. The line between required and advanced is the same one Common Core already draws: it is exactly the statistics CCSS expects of every graduate, and it directly operationalizes the ASA–NCTM position the AoE report cites (p.19): statistics should be "accessible to all students, not limited to advanced mathematics pathways."

**S-MD and S-CP.8–9** are appropriate for elective courses or courses that satisfy the data science requirements, but they do not need to be a part of every student’s pathway.

In Vermont's recommended requirements Algebra II is an *elective* math credit, not guaranteed for all. So, the typical Algebra II statistics standards (**S-ID.4 and all of S-IC**) cannot be assumed to be embedded into the required Algebra I + Geometry sequence as this would compress these courses. It always falls to the third credit, under either option.

**Meaningful coverage, not complete coverage.** Because Algebra II was not required for graduation in many Vermont high schools, coverage of the full statistics and probability standards was never guaranteed. Guaranteeing full coverage may limit the options for students as many data science courses lean toward the applied use of technology and may not allow for full coverage of the math standards. The full Algebra II statistics set, in particular the formal-inference standards S-IC.4 and S-IC.5 (developing a margin of error, and testing whether a difference is significant), should not be viewed as a requirement for a high school’s pathway. The goal for the credit is therefore substantial, central coverage of probability and statistics, not a complete checklist.

A widely used data science course makes the trade-off concrete. [California’s Introduction to Data Science (IDS)](https://www.introdatascience.org/wp-content/uploads/California-Common-Core-Mathematics-Standards-addressed-by-IDS.pdf) maps to S-ID.4 and S-IC.1, 2, 3, and 6, but not S-IC.4 or 5; it reaches inference informally without formalizing margin of error and significance. A faithful Algebra II course or AP Statistics does cover S-IC.4 and 5, and a strong data science course generally covers the rest. We therefore treat the formal-inference standards (S-IC.4–5) as content that some versions of this credit reach and others need not, rather than as a floor every qualifying course must clear.

# Elective Math Credit

The elective is the fourth math credit. Because every graduate’s required Common Core statistics is already guaranteed in the algebra, geometry, and statistics/data science credits, the elective does not need to carry any specific content checklist. That frees students to pursue courses where they can apply mathematical reasonings in domains that are relevant and interesting to them. This is the credit that lets a computer science course or a computationally rigorous career and technical education (CTE) program count toward the four required math credits, consistent with the AoE recommendation that the elective be defined “based on college/career pathway” (p.19) and that CTE learning satisfy content-area requirements where it aligns (SBR 2383.3).

Following the same approach used for the other credits, the elective is defined by a standards floor, not a list of approved courses. The floor is written to cast a deliberately wide net — while still requiring real mathematics, so that the credit means something.

## What qualifies as a math elective

A course satisfies the elective math credit when it provides students with sustained practice applying mathematical thinking applying concepts from the Common Core’s Standards for Mathematical Practice. This parallels the data science credit, which is defined by the statistical-investigation cycle being the central activity; here the central activity is mathematical reasoning over formal structures.

- **Central and sustained practice.** The Standards for Mathematical Practice, already adopted by the Vermont State Board of Education as part of the Common Core Math Standards, are the course’s primary, recurring mode of work across the year, not an incidental feature of an occasional lesson.

## CTE and Computer Science and STEAM

A computationally rigorous CS, CTE or STEAM course has two possible pathways into a math credit, depending on where its rigor actually lives.

- If the course’s central rigor is a **statistical or data investigation** (formulate → collect or consider → analyze → interpret real data), and it covers the required statistics and probability standards, it may count as either a **Statistics & Data Science credit** or an elective.

- If the course’s central rigor is **mathematical reasoning over formal structures** — algorithms, logic, functions and abstraction, algebraic or quantitative modeling, recursion, it should count as an **Elective Math credit**.

- If such reasoning is only **incidental**, the course uses numbers or tools but never asks students to build, trace, or justify a quantitative model or procedure, it is a valuable general elective but does not satisfy a math credit.

Only courses in which the mathematics components are incidental fail to qualify. Everything with sustained quantitative rigor lands in one math credit or the other.

## Illustrative Mathematics Algebra II

**Source**  
Course and unit materials: <https://im.kendallhunt.com/HS/teachers/3/index.html> (mirrored at curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org). EdReports review: <https://edreports.org/reports/overview/kendall-hunts-illustrative-mathematics-2021>

**Course Description**  
A full, standards-aligned Algebra 2 course and one of the more widely used open mathematics curricula in the country. It is openly licensed (CC BY 4.0), so every unit narrative, lesson, and assessment is free to view and adapt. EdReports rates IM 9–12 Math as meeting expectations across all three gateways. The course runs seven units — from sequences and functions through polynomial, exponential, and trigonometric functions — and closes with Unit 7, *Statistical Inferences*.

**Where the rigor lives**  
The year is sustained mathematical reasoning over formal structures, the work this credit names when it lists Algebra II as a qualifying course. For the statistics floor, the decisive unit is Unit 7, which builds from sampling to the normal distribution, margin of error, and the analysis of surveys, observational studies, and experiments using randomization distributions. That is the complete inference arc, including the formal pieces — margin of error and significance — that a conceptual data science course typically reaches only informally.

**Statistics and Data Science credit**  
Unit 7 of the course maps onto the third-credit statistics and probability standards:

| **Unit 7 lesson group**                                                                                                                 | **Standards**          |
|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------|
| Study Types — skepticism, surveys/observational/experiments, randomness in groups                                                       | S-IC.1, S-IC.3         |
| Distributions — describing data, normal distributions, areas under the curve                                                            | S-ID.4                 |
| Not All Samples Are the Same — variability, estimating proportions, reducing/interpreting margin of error, estimating a population mean | S-IC.4                 |
| Analyzing Experimental Data — randomization distributions, significance, questioning and evaluating studies                             | S-IC.2, S-IC.5, S-IC.6 |

Because Unit 7 reaches S-IC.4 and S-IC.5, IM Algebra 2 covers the two formal-inference standards that many data science courses skip. Conditional probability (S-CP) is not in this course; in IM's sequence it sits in Geometry, matching Appendix A and Option 1. Where a conceptual data science course delivers the investigation cycle but treats inference informally, a faithful Algebra 2 delivers the full inference cluster — both satisfy the credit, reaching the formal-inference standards differently.

**Course Description**

> Introduction to Data Science is a year-long, openly available course developed by UCLA's Center X and Department of Statistics under a National Science Foundation grant. It calls itself the leading national provider of high school data science education materials, and it is coding-intensive: students analyze real data by writing code in the R language using RStudio. The course runs four units:

- Data and Visualizations Distributions

- Probability, and Simulations

- Data Collection Methods

- Predictions and Models

> each organized around asking and answering statistical questions with real datasets.

**Where the rigor lives**

> The central, sustained activity is the data investigation itself: formulating questions, collecting or considering real data, analyzing it with code, and interpreting the results. That is the statistical-investigation arc that defines the data science door. IDS leans computational where YouCubed leans conceptual, but both treat inference informally. IDS reaches inference through simulation — deciding whether a model is consistent with a data-generating process (S-IC.2) — and through permutation-based informal inference (S-CP.9), rather than through a formal margin of error or a two-treatment significance test. This is the resampling tradition rather than the formula-and-table tradition. [datascience4everyone](https://www.datascience4everyone.org/high-school-curriculum-alignment)

**Statistics, Data Science, or Algebra II credit**

> IDS is a data science course, so it enters through the data-investigation door and can carry this credit. Its Common Core coverage, by the project's own unit alignment:

| **IDS unit**                                    | **Common Core statistics standards**       |
|-------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| 1 — Data and Visualizations                     | S-ID.1, 2, 3, 5, 6; S-IC.6                 |
| 2 — Distributions, Probability, and Simulations | S-ID.2, 3, 4; S-IC.2; S-CP.2, (+)9; S-IC.6 |
| 3 — Data Collection Methods                     | S-IC.1, 3; S-IC.6                          |
| 4 — Predictions and Models                      | S-ID.6, 7, 8; S-IC.2; S-IC.6               |

> In total, IDS covers S-ID.1–8, S-IC.1, 2, 3, and 6 (S-IC.6 recurs in every unit), and S-CP.2 with the advanced (+)9. It does not reach S-IC.4 (margin of error) or S-IC.5 (comparing two treatments for significance). Under Option 1, where the descriptive S-ID and conditional-probability S-CP standards are already embedded in Algebra and Geometry, IDS comfortably carries the third credit; the only floor standards it leaves unformalized are S-IC.4–5, which this credit treats as content some courses reach and others need not. Under Option 2, where the third credit must carry the full load on its own, IDS would not deliver the conditional-probability cluster (S-CP.1, 3–7) or the formal-inference standards, so a district choosing Option 2 would need to supplement. In either case, IDS also qualifies as the math elective.

## AP Computer Science A

**Source**

> <https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-computer-science-a>

**Course Description**

> *AP Computer Science A introduces students to computer science through programming. Fundamental topics in this course include the design of solutions to problems, the use of data structures to organize large sets of data, the development and implementation of algorithms to process data and discover new information, the analysis of potential solutions, and the ethical and social implications of computing systems. The course emphasizes object-oriented programming and design using the Java programming language.* – College Board

**Where the rigor lives**

> The central, year-long work is designing, writing, and analyzing algorithms: control flow and Boolean logic, iteration and accumulation, method abstraction with parameters and return values, and standard algorithms over collections of data. The 2025 revision adds file reading and working with data sets, but students manipulate that data structurally; they do not run a statistical investigation.
>
> The Standards for Mathematical Practice are the primary mode of work and are sustained all year: students make sense of problems and persevere, reason abstractly, construct and critique arguments (debugging is argument), make use of structure, and attend to precision. The content sits squarely in functions and abstraction, logic, and iteration.

**Math Elective**

> Because it has no statistical-investigation arc, it cannot count as a statistics or Data Science credit. A course this rigorous clearly earns a math credit, just not the data science one.

## AP Computer Science Principles

**Source**

> <https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-computer-science-principles>

**Course Description**

> AP Computer Science Principles introduces students to the breadth of the field of computer science. In this course, students will learn to design and evaluate solutions and to apply computer science to solve problems through the development of algorithms and programs. They will incorporate abstraction into programs and use data to discover new knowledge. Students will also explain how computing innovations and computing systems, including the Internet, work, explore their potential impacts, and contribute to a computing culture that is collaborative and ethical. It is important to note that the AP Computer Science Principles course does not have a designated programming language. Teachers have the flexibility to choose a programming language(s) that is most appropriate for their students to use in the classroom.

**Where the rigor lives**

> One big idea, algorithms and programming, the most heavily weighted at roughly a third of the exam, carries genuine mathematical reasoning: procedures and parameters, conditional logic, iteration, and abstraction. The other four big ideas are largely conceptual and civic: how networks work, data and privacy, and the societal impact of computing.

**Math Elective**

> Whether the practice is central and sustained depends on the local implementation. A version that foregrounds the algorithms-and-programming strand and keeps students building and reasoning about procedures across the year could count as a math elective. A survey-style version that treats algorithms and programming as one unit among five does not: the reasoning is present, but it is not the course’s central activity.
