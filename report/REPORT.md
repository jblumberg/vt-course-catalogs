# Vermont High School Course Catalogs and the Proposed Graduation Requirements

Nov 16, 2026 · @Josh Blumberg

## Executive summary

Vermont's public high schools are already close to offering what the proposed graduation requirements ask for. Nearly every school lists at least one course in every subject the proposals name. The harder question is which of those courses will **count**, and that depends on definitions the Agency of Education (AOE) and the State Board of Education (SBE) have not yet written.

To show how much those definitions matter, we looked at the 50 high schools whose course catalogs could be fully reviewed. We counted how many would offer every credit in the Complete Recommendation (Class of 2033 and later) under three different standards for what counts:

- If only courses that clearly match a requirement count, **6** of the 50 schools offer everything today.
- If AOE also accepts courses that are close and could qualify with a unit or two of added content, such as Environmental Science for Earth and Space Science or Integrated Math for Algebra I, the number rises to **21**.
- If AOE accepts any related course, it rises to **23**.

Under the Transitional Flexibility model (Classes of 2031 and 2032), the same three numbers are 14, 37 and 40.

This report is not a compliance review, and it does not argue for any particular definition. Its purpose is to lay out the decisions that remain open in each subject area, and to show which schools, by name, each decision would affect.

### The decisions that affect the most schools

1. **Health at 1.0 credit.** Today, 22 schools offer a single half-credit Health course, and Craftsbury offers a quarter credit. Of the 42 schools whose local requirements name Health, 33 require less than a full credit. The Transitional model's combined 1.5 credits of PE and Health would resolve this for every school except Blue Mountain, Craftsbury and Woodstock.
2. **Whether Environmental Science counts as Earth and Space Science.** Thirteen schools offer Earth and Space Science only through Environmental Science, AP Environmental Science, integrated science, ecology, forestry or agriculture courses. Whether those count depends on which Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) the Earth and Space credit will require, which is still to be defined. Spaulding has no Earth and Space course at all; its ninth-grade Physical Science course teaches "chemistry, earth science, and climate sciences" together. Champlain Valley, Missisquoi Valley and Woodstock offer only a single semester.
3. **Whether Integrated Math counts as Algebra I and Geometry.** Thirteen schools do not offer a stand-alone, one-year Algebra I course. Eleven of them (Arlington, BFA Fairfax, Burlington, Champlain Valley, Harwood, Mt. Abraham, Randolph, Richford, Rutland, South Burlington and Vergennes) teach an integrated or "Math 1 and Math 2" sequence instead. Hartford and Leland & Gray spread Algebra I over two courses. Until AOE publishes an equivalency rule, these schools cannot tell students and families whether their math courses meet the requirement.
4. **Room for electives, especially for career and technical education (CTE) students.** The Complete Recommendation leaves 3.0 credits for open electives, while the typical school requires 7.0 elective credits today. A two-year CTE program usually earns 6 to 8 high school credits. CTE students will be able to finish on time only if the credits embedded in their programs are mapped to the new requirement categories, for example to Physical, Life or Earth and Space Science rather than to "science" in general.
5. **Whether Civics must be its own course.** Montpelier, Hartford, Middlebury, Otter Valley and Springfield offer Civics content only within other courses, such as social movements, law, IB Global Politics or combined history and civics. South Burlington and Stowe offer one semester.
6. **What a credit means.** Five schools award diplomas based on proficiency alone (BFA Fairfax, Harwood, Mt. Mansfield, Twinfield and U-32). On the block schedules used at Brattleboro and the three Quarry Valley schools, a one-semester course earns a full credit. As a result, 22.5 credits does not represent the same amount of learning or class time in every building.

### How the two proposals compare

| What counts toward a requirement | Complete Recommendation (Class of 2033 and later): schools offering every requirement | Transitional Flexibility (Classes of 2031 and 2032) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Strict:** only courses that clearly match the requirement today | 6 of 50 | 14 of 50 |
| **Moderate:** also courses that are close and could qualify with a unit or two of added content | 21 of 50 | 37 of 50 |
| **Loose:** also courses where the content is embedded in a broader course, below grade level, or a support course | 23 of 50 | 40 of 50 |

The Transitional model is easier to meet for three reasons. It combines PE and Health into 1.5 credits, it moves Statistics or Algebra II into electives, and it does not require a world language.

### What course catalogs can and cannot tell us

A course catalog shows what a school **offers**. It does not show whether a course actually runs in a given year, how many sections run, or how many students enroll. A course offered every other year to a handful of students looks the same in a catalog as a core course with eight sections. Every count in this report is a count of offerings. Joining this work with **Ed-Fi** section and enrollment data is the natural next step, and until then "offers" should be read as "lists in its catalog."

## Purpose, audience and sources

This report uses each public high school's own course catalog to show where the proposed graduation requirements raise difficult questions. It was written for Dr. Erin Davis's team at the Agency of Education, for members of the State Board of Education, and for AOE's content specialists in mathematics, science and social studies. We hope it is also useful to school leaders who will eventually put the requirements into practice.

### The two proposals

The report compares two proposals described in AOE's memo to the State Board of October 20, 2026. Both total 22.5 credits; they differ in how those credits are divided. Draft 8 of the State Board's rule (Exhibit 1) matches the Complete Recommendation.

### Which schools are included

The study covers Vermont's 56 public high schools. Career and technical education centers, independent schools and town academies are outside its scope.

- We found catalogs for 54 schools, listing 5,566 school courses and 495 CTE programs. Enosburg Falls and Williamstown do not publish a catalog.
- **Fifty schools could be fully reviewed.** Four could not:
  - Oxbow's catalog is a Canva design that does not include a course list.
  - Mill River and Rivendell list course titles without descriptions.
  - The catalog we found for Cabot covers only 16 courses.

  These four schools appear in Appendix A as "data incomplete." Missing information about them is never reported as a gap.

### How each course was judged

We compared each course description with the requirement definitions in AOE's documents and assigned one of two ratings:

- **Clearly qualifies ("strong"):** the description plainly covers the content of the requirement.
- **Possibly qualifies ("possible"):** the course is close, but whether it counts depends on a definition AOE has not yet written.

When a catalog says what requirement a course fulfills, we recorded that claim separately, and a claim on its own never closes a gap. Before applying this method to every school, we checked it against course-by-course ratings made by a person. The two agreed on 85% of 97 pilot courses and on 93% of 30 courses held back for testing.

## Theme 1 — Subjects missing entirely from a school

Only two of the 50 schools we could fully review are missing a required subject altogether. Canaan offers no world language, and Spaulding offers no Earth and Space Science course. That is encouraging. A more widespread concern is that many schools meet a requirement through a single course, often taught by a single teacher, so one staffing change could open a gap.

### What the catalogs show

| School (2024-25 enrollment) | Missing subject | Closest course the school offers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Canaan Schools (184 students, grades 7 to 12) | World Language or American Sign Language | *Art of World Cultures*, which is an art course rather than language study |
| Spaulding Union High School (610 students) | Earth and Space Science | *Physical Science* (grade 9), which teaches "the basics of chemistry, earth science, and climate sciences" in one course |
| Cabot School (150 students) | Financial Literacy, Civics, and Earth and Space Science | Unknown: our copy of the catalog lists only 16 courses |
| Rivendell Academy (209 students) | Financial Literacy, Civics, and Earth and Space Science | Unknown: the catalog lists titles only |

Spaulding's ninth-grade course is a good illustration of how much depends on definitions. Its description says students will study "thermodynamics in the earth system" and "molecular processes in the earth's system," and will work on "how to solve problems from sea level rise from global melting in an arctic community." The course already contains Earth science. Whether it can count toward the Earth and Space requirement depends on whether AOE allows one integrated course to divide its credit between Physical Science and Earth and Space Science (see Theme 5).

### Requirements that rest on a single course

At many schools, one course is the only way to meet a requirement. If that course stops running, the school has a gap.

| Requirement | Schools with exactly one qualifying course | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Civics | 26 | Proctor (116 students) and Winooski (182) each have one *Civics* course; at Craftsbury the only Civics course is *AP U.S. Government* |
| Earth and Space Science | 19 | Hazen's *Astronomy and Rocket Science*, Peoples Academy's *Astronomy*, Randolph's *Integrated Science III: Space Physics*, Otter Valley's *Geophysics* |
| Health | 15 | Usually a single required Health or Wellness course |
| World Studies | 9 | Poultney, Proctor and West Rutland share one *Global Studies* course |

Two patterns stand out.

- **Some schools rely on a narrow Earth and Space course.** At several schools the only Earth and Space course is Astronomy. Astronomy addresses the NGSS standards for Earth's place in the universe, but not those for Earth's systems or for Earth and human activity. If AOE requires the full range of Earth and Space standards, these schools would have a gap.
- **Some schools depend on an AP course.** At Craftsbury, Lake Region and Mt. Mansfield, the only course that clearly qualifies as Civics is AP U.S. Government. Requiring Civics of every student would mean either enrolling every student in an AP course or creating a new course.

### The decision ahead

AOE will need to decide whether access to a course is enough. May a school meet a requirement through the Vermont Virtual Learning Cooperative (VTVLC), dual enrollment, or a partnership with a neighboring school? The answer matters most for Canaan's world language requirement and for small schools that run a single section of a required course.

**What to keep in mind:** catalogs do not say how often a course runs. Ed-Fi section data would show which of these single-course requirements are served by just one section a year.

## Theme 2 — Today's local graduation requirements compared with the proposals

Most schools would need to require more Health, more mathematics, more arts and a world language, while leaving less room for electives. At the same time, the proposed total of 22.5 credits is lower than what most schools require today. Of the 42 schools that state a total, 34 require more than 22.5 credits, and the typical school requires 24. Only five require less: Champlain Valley, Craftsbury, Hazen and Twin Valley (22.0 each), and Northfield (21.5).

### Where local requirements fall short of the Complete Recommendation

- **Mathematics (4.0 credits).** Only nine schools require four math credits today: Bellows Falls, Blue Mountain, Canaan, Lake Region, Leland & Gray, Peoples Academy, and the three Quarry Valley schools (Poultney, Proctor and West Rutland). Most schools require three, which matches the Transitional model.
- **Health (1.0 credit).** Thirty-three of 42 schools require a half credit. The nine that already require a full credit are Bellows Falls, Blue Mountain, Middlebury, Peoples Academy, Poultney, Proctor, Springfield, West Rutland and Winooski.
- **Arts (1.5 credits).** Thirty-seven of 45 schools require one credit.
- **World Language (1.0 credit).** Fourteen schools require a world language today: Bellows Falls, Burlington, Craftsbury, Essex, Green Mountain, Middlebury, Mill River, Milton, Poultney, Proctor, Springfield, West Rutland, Windsor and Winooski.
- **Electives.** Every school that sets an elective requirement asks for more than the Complete Recommendation's 3.0 credits. The typical school requires 7.0, and the range is 3.5 to 10. Twenty-five schools require more than the Transitional model's 6.0.

### Science and social studies: the same totals, divided differently

In science and social studies, most schools already require the proposed number of credits. Thirty-nine of 47 schools require three science credits, and 29 of 46 require three in social studies. What changes is how those credits are divided.

- **Science.** A typical local requirement today is Biology plus two more science courses. The proposals instead require one credit each in Physical Science, Life Science, and Earth and Space Science. Some catalogs also sort courses differently than the proposals do. Essex and Hartford, for example, both count *Earth & Space Science* toward their local physical science requirement.
- **Social studies.** Many schools require "Global Citizenship" or "Social Studies" credits and name only U.S. History as a specific course. Some are already moving toward the proposals: Danville will require a semester of Civics beginning with the Class of 2029, and Mt. Anthony will require one credit of "Civics / Financial Literacy" beginning with the same class.

### Local requirements the proposals would crowd out

Twenty schools have a local requirement that has no place in either proposal. With only 3.0 open elective credits, each of these would have to compete with every other elective.

- **Technology and computer science.** Richford requires a full credit of Computer Science and a half credit of Consumer Economics. Rutland requires a full credit of Information Technology and a half credit of Life Skills. Milton, Mt. Anthony and Vergennes each require a half credit of Technology; Canaan and Essex require a half credit of Computer Applications; and South Burlington requires a half credit of Information Technology.
- **Capstones and service.** Windsor requires a Junior Seminar and Capstone (2.0 credits). Champlain Valley requires its RISE program (2.0 credits) and a Senior Exit Experience. Blue Mountain requires a Capstone (1.0 credit), Middlebury requires Theory of Knowledge, and Lake Region and Milton require community service or internships.
- **Business.** Missisquoi Valley requires one credit of Business Education.

### What a credit means

The comparisons above assume that one credit means the same thing everywhere. In practice it does not.

- **Brattleboro** counts 96 of its own units, with a semester block course worth 4 units. That is roughly equivalent to 24 standard credits.
- **Poultney, Proctor, West Rutland and Peoples Academy** award a full credit for a one-semester course. Their totals of 28 and 34 reflect their block schedules, not twice as much coursework.
- **Danville** counts semesters, and requires 42 of them.
- **Harwood, Randolph, South Burlington, Winooski and Woodstock** do not state a credit total. At the five schools that award diplomas on proficiency alone, credit counts play a secondary role.

**The decision ahead:** before 22.5 credits can be compared fairly across schools, the proposals will need a shared definition of a credit, whether based on time in class, on demonstrated proficiency, or on both.

## Theme 3 — Courses that "possibly" qualify: where AOE sets the bar

Of all the choices ahead, where AOE sets the bar for a course to count will affect the most schools. Under the Complete Recommendation, it is the difference between 6 schools and 23 schools being able to offer every requirement. If the bar is set too low, the new requirements will not raise expectations. If it is set too high, many creative and interdisciplinary courses, and courses designed for students who are not yet ready for the standard course, would no longer count, and some students could find it harder to graduate on time.

Across all schools, we found 1,251 cases where a course is close to a requirement but not clearly inside it. We rated these "possibly qualifies." To see how much they matter, we tested three ways of treating them:

| Approach | What counts | Complete Recommendation: schools offering every requirement | Transitional Flexibility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Strict** | Only courses whose description clearly covers the requirement | 6 | 14 |
| **Moderate** | Also courses that are close and could qualify with a unit or two of added content. Examples are Environmental Science for Earth and Space, Integrated Math or a two-part Algebra I, interdisciplinary courses, applied and technical courses, and courses whose grade level isn't stated. | 21 | 37 |
| **Loose** | Also courses where the content is part of a broader course, courses below grade level, and support courses | 23 | 40 |

It is important to remember that these ratings are based only on course descriptions, and descriptions are easy to change. A teacher can add or drop a unit or two in a full-year course, so many "possible" courses could qualify after a modest revision. **The Moderate approach describes what most schools could reach with reasonable effort.**

The Strict approach would put pressure on three different kinds of courses at once:

- courses that are **creative or interdisciplinary**, such as Windsor's *Earth & the Environment* and Montpelier's *Social Change Theory and Practice*;
- courses **built around a career or a place**, such as Canaan's *EC Forestry Resources* and Danville's *Woodworking*;
- courses that **may not be rigorous enough**, such as Lake Region's *Consumer Mathematics*.

Only the third group is the intended target of higher standards. A definition that cannot tell these groups apart risks losing the first two along with the third.

### Examples from course descriptions

**Earth and Space Science (Moderate): Windsor's *Earth & the Environment*.** This is a full-year course for tenth graders, and Windsor's catalog says it meets the science requirement.

> "a full year, interdisciplinary science course … dig deeper into topics such as: Sustainability, Basic Earth Science, Waste Management and Recycling, Green Building, Energy, Air-Water-Soil Quality, Climate Change, Agriculture, and Population Growth."

Earth science is one topic in a list of nine. Adding a unit or two on topics such as plate tectonics and the rock cycle could make the course qualify. Whether that would be enough depends on which NGSS Earth and Space standards AOE attaches to the credit.

**Earth and Space Science (Moderate): Craftsbury's *AP Environmental Science*.** This is Craftsbury's only course with Earth science content.

> "Students investigate ecological processes, energy and resource use, population dynamics, biodiversity, pollution, climate change, and the impacts of human activities on Earth's systems. Through laboratory investigations …"

AP Environmental Science thoroughly covers the NGSS standards on Earth and human activity. It touches on Earth's systems and covers little about space.

**Civics (Moderate): Montpelier's *Social Change Theory and Practice*.** This full-credit course is Montpelier's closest match to Civics.

> "In the spring, the students will identify a current policy issue at the district, city, or state level and work together on a class-led campaign."

This is applied civic action, which many would consider the heart of civics education, but the description does not cover the structure of government. Under the Strict approach, Montpelier would need to add a course.

**Financial Literacy (Loose only): Fair Haven's *Adulting 101*.** This is a half-credit course for juniors and seniors.

> "determine the impact of credit cards and loans on their credit score, budget, save and invest their money, and file their taxes. Students will also learn how to manage their time, balance their physical and mental health …"

The memo calls for "stand-alone content." Fair Haven, Mt. Mansfield and Randolph offer Financial Literacy only as part of broader courses like this one.

### Mathematics below Algebra I

Thirty-seven schools list a total of 62 math courses that come before Algebra I, with titles such as Pre-Algebra, Essentials, Foundations, Consumer Math, Applied Math and Math in the Workplace.

- **Lake Region** offers four, including a three-year sequence that comes before Algebra I. *Essentials of Mathematics I* is "a review of the operations on whole numbers, fractions, decimals, and percentages." *Essentials of Mathematics II* covers "banking, budget planning, insurance, taxes." *Consumer Mathematics* is "designed as a third year offering for those completing Essentials of Mathematics II." Lake Region also offers *Pre-Algebra*.
- **Blue Mountain, Burlington, Fair Haven and Rutland** each offer three such courses, and Danville offers *Pre-Algebra* and *Math in the Workplace*.

In this report, Pre-Algebra counts as a math elective (the Math Pathway credit). Consumer and Essentials math courses are rated "possible" because they may be below high school level; that is a question for a math specialist.

**Why this matters for students:** the Complete Recommendation requires four specific math credits: Algebra I, Geometry, Statistics or Algebra II, and a Math Pathway course. A student who begins in a course like Lake Region's Essentials sequence has room for only one course below Algebra I and must start Algebra I by tenth grade to finish on time. Catalogs do not show how many students are in these courses; Ed-Fi enrollment data would show the size of the challenge.

### When a catalog's claim and the course description disagree

For 77 courses (111 claims in all), what the catalog says a course fulfills is not supported by its description. There are two main reasons.

1. **Science credit is usually generic today.** Windsor's CTE listings say "Embedded credit: Science." Essex lists *Planetary Astronomy* and *Earth and Space Science* as earning "Physical Science credit." Under the proposals, each of these would need to name a specific area: Physical, Life, or Earth and Space.
2. **Credit is awarded by equivalency.**
   - Danville's *Woodworking* lists "Credit areas: English, Science, Elective," but its description centers on "teamwork, leadership, professionalism, craftsmanship."
   - Lake Region's *Programming for the Web in HTML* "may fulfill 1/2 credit of the fine arts graduation requirement."
   - Springfield's *Carpentry II* lists "Embedded credits: 1 Math, 1 Art, 1 Elective."

**The decision ahead:** will AOE require that credit earned by equivalency be supported by documented standards in the course? If so, the 19 schools that make claims like these, most often Fair Haven, Hazen, Richford, Springfield and Windsor, will need to revise their course documents.

### Labs

Each of the three science requirements says "with lab," but no AOE document defines what a lab is. Of 664 science courses, 418 (63%) mention labs or hands-on investigation; the descriptions of the other 246 do not say either way. A definition would settle whether those 246 courses count. It could address how much lab time is required, what kinds of investigation qualify, and whether field work or simulations count.

## Theme 4 — Electives: less room than schools offer today

The Complete Recommendation leaves 3.0 credits for electives that students choose freely. Today the typical school requires 7.0 elective credits, and 25 schools require more than even the Transitional model's 6.0. In practice, students would have less room for courses such as psychology, engineering, business, a second world language, or a third year of art. The effect would be greatest for students in two-year career and technical education programs.

### What would compete for 3.0 elective credits

**Courses that don't count toward any requirement.** Of the 5,318 regular courses in the catalogs, 419 (8%) do not fit any of the proposed requirements. They are concentrated in a few areas:

| Type of course | Number of courses | Examples (school) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Social science electives | 135 | *AP Psychology* and *Sociology* (Peoples Academy); *Introduction to Gender Studies* and *Great Ideas in Philosophy* (Essex); *Archaeology* and *Economic Justice PBL* (Randolph) |
| Technology and engineering | 89 | *Design & Engineering Technology 1–3* (Champlain Valley); *Forged with Fire* and *Ultimate Maker Space* (Peoples Academy); *Small Engines* and *Welding Basics* (Otter Valley) |
| Business | 46 | *Microsoft Excel* (Lake Region); *Entrepreneurship* (Essex); *Business Law* (Champlain Valley) |
| Family and consumer science | 28 | *International Foods* (Burlington and Essex) |
| Computer science | 20 | *Web Design* (Burlington); *Programming for the Web in CSS* (Lake Region) |
| Other, including media, design, capstones and driver education | 101 | *AI and Society* and *Intro to Design Thinking* (Champlain Valley) |

**Courses beyond what a requirement asks for.** Every course a student takes after meeting a requirement also uses elective room, such as a fifth English course, a second world language, a third arts course, or AP Physics after Chemistry. These are often the courses that give each school its character. Examples include Lake Region's wide range of half-credit English electives and Montpelier's place-based courses, such as *La Bonne Crêpe* (a student-run food cart) and *Manufacturing & Student Entrepreneurship*.

### The effect on career and technical education students

This is one of the clearest implementation challenges in the report. Where catalogs list a credit value, CTE programs typically earn 3 to 4 high school credits a year (83 of the 100 listings that show a value). Most of that credit is recorded as elective credit today, as these examples show:

- **Richford, Construction Technology:** "Year 1: 4 elective credit; Year 2: 1 math credit, 1 practical art credit, 2 elective credit."
- **Mt. Mansfield, Automotive Science and Technology I:** "Year 1 – 2.0 Elective, 0.5 Science, 0.5 English."
- **Hartford, Information Technology:** "1 Technology credit, 0.5 English credit, 1.5 elective credits."

The math is straightforward. A two-year program earns 6 to 8 credits, and the Complete Recommendation already requires 19.5 credits in named subjects. If most of the program's credit counts as electives, a CTE student would need 25.5 to 27.5 credits to graduate, which is 3 to 5 more than the 22.5 total. That student can finish on time only if one of two things is true:

1. the school's schedule has room for 25.5 credits or more (catalogs rarely say; see Systems context), or
2. the credits embedded in the CTE program count toward named requirements.

Embedded credit is the realistic path, but it will need to be tied to the new categories. "Embedded science" (as at Windsor and Fair Haven) would need to become Physical, Life, or Earth and Space Science. "Embedded math" would need to say whether it counts as Algebra I, Geometry, or the Math Pathway credit.

Seventeen schools' catalogs already describe embedded or core credit for CTE programs: Bellows Falls, Blue Mountain, Canaan, Fair Haven, Hartford, Hazen, Lamoille, Middlebury, Milton, Missisquoi Valley, Montpelier, Mt. Mansfield, Richford, South Burlington, Springfield, Windsor and Woodstock. Many others list CTE programs by name only, and some, including Leland & Gray, Spaulding, Vergennes and Winooski, do not list them at all.

### The decisions ahead

1. Will AOE publish a statewide crosswalk from CTE program standards to the new credit categories, or will each school and CTE center work this out on its own?
2. Should a required credit earned at a CTE center count only if the center's course documents name the AOE standards it covers?
3. Should students who complete a CTE program have more room for electives?

**What to keep in mind:** catalogs list the electives a school offers, not how many students take them. Ed-Fi data would show which electives actually run and how many students would lose access.

## Theme 5 — Interdisciplinary courses and partial credit

Twenty-four schools already offer a total of 42 interdisciplinary courses, and they award credit for them in four different ways. The proposals do not yet say how one course can count toward more than one requirement. Without that guidance, some of the state's most innovative courses would become the hardest to record on a transcript.

### Four ways schools award interdisciplinary credit today

| Approach | Schools and courses | How the credit works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A double-length course that earns two credits** | Champlain Valley's *Core Humanities* (grade 9) and *Grade 10 Humanities – American Studies*; Colchester's *The American Experience*; Essex's *Grade 9 Honors Humanities*; Otter Valley's *Humanities 9*; Rutland's *American Voices*; South Burlington's and Bellows Falls's *American Studies* | Two credits: one in English and one in social studies |
| **One course split between two subjects** | BFA St. Albans's *PBL Playwriting & Performance*; South Burlington's *Holocaust Studies*; Proctor's *Comics & Culture* | Half credit in each subject, for example "half a credit in English and half a credit in Art in one semester" |
| **The student chooses** | Windsor's *The Making of the Modern World*; Vergennes's *Humanities*; Missisquoi Valley's *Humanities in Action*; Middlebury's *DP Environmental Systems and Societies* | The student decides which subject receives the credit |
| **A program that earns several credits** | Champlain Valley's *Project Academy*, which earns four credits, "one in each of English, Math, Social Studies, and Science"; Fair Haven's *Outdoor Pursuits*, which earns three (math, science and PE); Harwood's *Community Learning Center* | Several credits earned through one block of time |

Some catalogs do not say how credit is divided, including Twinfield's *ELA and SCI Integrated Studies*, Danville's *STEM and LIT*, Mt. Mansfield's *Vermont Sustainability Project* and Lamoille's *History & Science of Mummification*.

**An example: Twinfield's *10th Grade Human Impact on the Biosphere / ELA Integrated*.** This is a full-year course, and the catalog does not state its credit.

> "Students will then investigate cells, disease, and the ways human activity has influenced the development, spread, and treatment of infectious and noninfectious diseases. The course concludes with an in-depth study of climate change …"

This single course touches Introductory English, Life Science (cells and disease), and Earth and Space Science (climate change). If a course can count toward only one subject, Twinfield loses its path to Life Science, which is why Twinfield shows Life Science as "possible" only.

### Half-credit courses are common, but not in every subject

The share of courses offered for a half credit varies widely by subject:

| Subject | Share of courses offered for a half credit |
| --- | --- |
| PE and Health | 76% |
| Arts | 58% |
| English language arts | 37% |
| Social studies | 35% |
| Science | 20% |
| Mathematics | 10% |
| World language | 3% |

Half-credit courses help smaller schools offer variety. Lake Region's English electives and Missisquoi Valley's one-semester *CP Earth and Space Science* are examples. A requirement of one credit in Earth and Space Science can be met with two semester courses. Champlain Valley, Missisquoi Valley and Woodstock, however, offer only one semester of Earth and Space, so they currently fall a half credit short.

### Integrated science and CTE programs as partial credit

- **Ninth-grade integrated science.** Green Mountain's *Integrated Science 9*, Milton's *Physical & Earth Science*, Mt. Abraham's *Science 9: Physical Sciences & Earth Systems*, U-32's *9th Grade Earth/Physical Science* and Spaulding's *Physical Science* each combine physical science and Earth science. If a course could award a half credit in each, these five schools would be halfway to the Earth and Space credit. Each would still need a second half credit, for example from an environmental science or astronomy elective.
- **CTE programs.** Thirteen schools' catalogs state embedded core credit for 133 CTE programs. Springfield (with River Valley Technical Center) and Windsor give the most detail, for example "Embedded credits: 1 Math, 1 Science, 1 English."

### The decision ahead

AOE will need a rule for how one course can count toward more than one subject. Three options stand out:

1. **Whole credit only.** Each course counts toward one subject. This is simple to check, but it would disadvantage the 24 schools above and most CTE programs.
2. **Declared fractions.** The school states the split in the course documents, such as a half credit in each of two subjects, and shows which standards each part addresses. BFA St. Albans and South Burlington already work this way.
3. **Proficiency-based credit.** Credit follows the proficiencies a student demonstrates. This fits the five schools that award diplomas on proficiency alone, but it is the hardest to compare across schools.

This report does not address whether awarding several credits for one course could weaken rigor. That question deserves its own study.

## Theme 6 — Integrated Math as Algebra I and Geometry

The proposals name Algebra I and Geometry as specific courses. Eleven Vermont high schools do not teach them as separate courses, and two more spread Algebra I across two years. Until AOE publishes a rule on what counts as equivalent, these 13 schools cannot tell families whether their core math sequence satisfies the requirement.

### Schools affected

| School | Math sequence in the catalog | Algebra I today | Geometry today |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arlington | *Math 1* and *Math 2*; *Math A* and *Math B Honors* | Possible only | Possible only |
| Harwood | *Integrated Math 1* and *2* | Possible only | Possible only |
| Mt. Abraham | *Integrated Math 1* and *2* | Possible only | Possible only |
| Richford | *Integrated Math I* and *II* | Possible only | Possible only |
| Rutland | *Integrated Math 1* through *4*, college prep and honors | Possible only | Possible only |
| South Burlington | *Math 1*, *Math 1 by 2 Years* and *Math 2* | Possible only | Possible only |
| Burlington | *Integrated Math 1* and *2* | Possible only | Covered by another course |
| BFA Fairfax | *Math 1: Linear and Exponential Functions*; *Math 2: Quadratic Functions* | Possible only | Covered |
| Champlain Valley | *Core Math 1 Foundations*, *Core Math 1* and *Math 2* | Possible only | Covered |
| Randolph | *AGS 1* (Algebra, Geometry and Statistics) | Possible only | Covered |
| Vergennes | *Math 1* and *Math 2* | Possible only | Covered |

| Leland & Gray | *Algebra I Part 1* and *Part 2* | Possible only (two-part course) | Covered |

Fair Haven, Montpelier, Mt. Mansfield and Poultney also offer integrated courses, but they offer a traditional Algebra I course as well.

**An example: Burlington's *Integrated Math 1*** (one credit, grades 9 and 10).

> "Univariate statistics, Understanding linear functions and their applications, Linear regression, Systems of linear equations and inequalities, Coordinate geometry, Rigid transformations, Triangle congruence, and Exponential functions."

About two-thirds of this course is Algebra I content. The rest (transformations, congruence and coordinate geometry) is Geometry, and the statistics strand belongs to neither requirement. Burlington's catalog says only that "one of the three recommended credits for graduation is Integrated Math."

**An example: Hartford's *Algebra 1 – Part A*.**

> "an emphasis on Solving, Graphing, and Writing Linear Equations and Inequalities, as well as introduce Exponential Functions."

Part A covers the first half of Algebra I and is offered for a full credit. A student who completes both parts has finished Algebra I, but has used two credits to do so.

### Three possible rules and their effects

| Rule | Schools where Algebra I is available (of 50) | Schools where Geometry is available | What would happen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Integrated and two-part courses do not count** | 37 | 44 | Thirteen schools would need to add traditional courses or rebuild their math sequence |
| **Full credit:** Integrated Math 1 counts as Algebra I, Integrated Math 2 counts as Geometry, and Parts A and B together count as Algebra I | 50 | 50 | No school would need to change, but this overstates the match, because content is redistributed across the sequence |
| **Split credit:** Integrated Math 1 and Integrated Math 2 each count as a half credit of Algebra I plus a half credit of Geometry | 50 | 50, for students who finish both courses | Students who finish both courses end up in the same place as under full credit. Students who stop after Math 1, or who transfer, would hold a half credit of each |

The split-credit rule reflects how integrated sequences are designed. The Common Core's integrated pathway spreads Algebra I, Geometry and Algebra II content across three courses (Math I, II and III). It raises two questions for AOE's math specialist:

1. **Do Math 1 and Math 2 together cover all of the Geometry standards?** In the Common Core integrated pathway, some Geometry topics, such as circles and some modeling, appear in Math III. Rutland describes its *Integrated Math 4* as "heavy geometry, trig, circles." A strict match to the standards could require Math III before the Geometry credit is awarded.
2. **How should two-year Algebra I courses count?** Hartford, Leland & Gray, and South Burlington's *Math 1 by 2 Years* could award two credits for one year's worth of Algebra I. The second credit might count as the Math Pathway elective, or as nothing at all.

### The decision ahead

AOE could publish an equivalency table that addresses:

- Integrated Math 1, 2 and 3;
- Algebra I taught over two years;
- Algebra I "Foundations" courses and support courses, such as Champlain Valley's *Core Math 1 Foundations* and the *Strategic Algebra* courses at Colchester and Montpelier;
- the statistics strand within Integrated Math 1, and whether it counts toward Statistics and Data Science.

The table should also make clear whether equivalency is decided course by course or standard by standard.

## How schools are organized today

Course catalogs are the main tool students and families use to plan four years of high school, and most are not yet set up for a system of specific requirement categories. This section describes the starting point that any rollout would build on.

### What catalogs tell students

| Information | Share of courses where the catalog provides it |
| --- | --- |
| A course description | 97% |
| Credit value | 71% |
| Grade levels | 71% |
| Length (semester or year) | 69% |
| Prerequisites | 46% |
| **Which graduation requirement the course meets** | **23%** |

A few schools already do this well. Stowe (92% of courses), Windsor (89%), Essex (85%) and Leland & Gray (82%) state which requirement each course meets, and U-32 (77%) lists the proficiencies each course addresses. At the other end, Cabot, Craftsbury, Mill River, Poultney, Rivendell, Proctor, Fair Haven and North Country state this for 2% of courses or fewer.

### How schools grade and award diplomas

| Approach | Schools |
| --- | --- |
| A combination of credits and proficiencies | 43 |
| Proficiency only | 5: BFA Fairfax, Harwood, Mt. Mansfield, Twinfield and U-32 |
| Credits only | 4: Otter Valley, Oxbow, Peoples Academy and Stowe (Stowe moved from a 4-point proficiency scale to a 100-point scale in 2025-26) |
| Not stated | 2: Cabot and Rivendell |

At schools that award diplomas on proficiency alone, a credit is a way of reporting progress on proficiencies rather than a measure of time in class. A requirement such as "one credit of Earth and Space Science with lab" would need to be translated into proficiencies before these schools could apply it.

### Schedules

Few catalogs describe the school schedule in detail. Only 19 of 54 say how many courses a student takes at one time, and 20 do not describe the schedule at all. Seven schools use an alternating-day (A/B) block schedule and five use a 4×4 block schedule. We were able to estimate how much of a four-year schedule local requirements take up at only 15 schools, and four of those estimates came out above 100%, which is not possible. For that reason, this report does not publish schedule-capacity figures until they can be verified. These figures matter for CTE students (see Theme 4), because whether a student can earn 25.5 credits or more depends on schedule capacity that no catalog states clearly.

### Career and technical education and flexible pathways

- **Catalogs describe CTE programs in very different ways.** Some give full descriptions with the core credits each program earns (Springfield with River Valley Technical Center, and Twin Valley with Windham Regional Career Center). Many list programs by name only, and some (Leland & Gray, Spaulding, Vergennes and Winooski) do not mention them at all. As a result, counts of CTE programs in this report reflect how catalogs are written, not how many programs students can reach.
- **Flexible pathways** include independent study, work-based learning, dual enrollment and virtual courses. Forty-nine schools list at least one, with the most at Mt. Abraham (16), Colchester (14), Milton (12), and Brattleboro, Essex and Harwood (11 each). Because catalogs do not link these pathways to particular requirements, this report never uses one to fill a gap.
- **The decision ahead:** may a flexible pathway satisfy a specific requirement, such as Civics earned through a work-based learning placement? If so, what documentation should be required?

## Methods and limitations

Every number in this report counts courses that schools list in their catalogs, judged from course descriptions against requirement definitions that AOE has not yet finalized. The counts are best read as "how many schools would be affected by each decision," not as measures of compliance.

### How the information was gathered

1. **Catalogs.** We found and downloaded catalogs for 54 of the 56 schools and recorded the date each was retrieved.
   - Forty-four are for the 2026-27 school year.
   - Nine are older: Bellows Falls (2024-26); Cabot, Canaan, Green Mountain, Leland & Gray, Mt. Anthony and North Country (2025-26); and the undated Quarry Valley district catalog.
   - Windsor's 2026-27 catalog may be a draft.
2. **Courses.** We recorded 6,061 courses with their descriptions copied word for word, and checked each description against the original catalog automatically.
3. **Ratings.** We compared each course with each requirement based on its content and rated it "clearly qualifies" or "possibly qualifies," with a written reason. What a catalog says a course fulfills was recorded separately and never used on its own to fill a gap. Our ratings agreed with ratings made by a person on 85% of 97 pilot courses (from Danville and Lake Region) and 93% of 30 courses from Montpelier that were held back for testing.
4. **How coverage was counted.**
   - A requirement is met when clearly qualifying courses offer at least the required number of credits. Where a catalog does not list credits, it is met when there are at least as many courses as credits required.
   - CTE center programs and flexible pathways are never used to fill a gap.
   - Courses offered every other year **are** counted, because a student can still take them within four years. Requirements met only by such a course are noted.
5. **Corrections made while preparing this report.**
   - Essex's *Algebra I 100* and *200* now count as full Algebra I courses; the numbers are course levels, not parts of a sequence.
   - Courses offered every other year are now included. This changed Winooski's Earth and Space Science from a gap to "possible."
   - Cabot is treated as having incomplete data.
   - Brattleboro's units were divided by 4 to convert them to standard credits.
   - Green Mountain's requirements use the Class of 2028 table.

### Limitations

- **Offerings, not enrollment.** A course in the catalog may not run in a given year. Joining this work with **Ed-Fi** section and enrollment data is the recommended next step. Ed-Fi data would show:
  - how many students take courses below Algebra I;
  - how many additional sections of Health or Civics would be needed;
  - which electives actually run.
- **Descriptions are brief.** Most are two to five sentences. A course rated "possible" may fully meet the standard in practice, and teachers can add or drop a unit in any year.
- **Requirement definitions are still provisional.** Credit amounts follow the memo of October 20, 2026. We assumed one credit each for Civics, U.S. History and World Studies, and "lab" and "stand-alone" financial literacy are not yet defined.
- **Some catalogs are incomplete.**
  - Oxbow: no course list.
  - Mill River and Rivendell: course titles only.
  - Cabot: a partial catalog.
  - North Country and Twinfield: partial, or read from web pages.
  - Woodstock: no graduation requirements section.
- **Outside this report's scope:** the catalogs of CTE centers themselves, independent schools and town academies, and whether awarding several credits for one course could weaken rigor (a question for a later study).
